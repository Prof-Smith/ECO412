## Purpose of the field study

Your objective is not to determine whether people are rational or irrational. Identify how a choice is presented, document what is directly observable, develop a possible behavioral explanation, consider an alternative explanation, and state what the observation cannot establish.

## Step 1: Select a decision environment

Choose a setting in which a person must notice, compare, select, continue, decline, purchase, register, dispose of something, or take no action.

<div class="caw-examples"><article><small>CAMPUS OR WORKPLACE</small><p>Registration form, appointment screen, student-service page, sign-up process, directional sign, queue, service counter, or dining display.</p></article><article><small>CONSUMER SETTING</small><p>Menu, subscription page, pricing display, checkout screen, product comparison, return process, or loyalty invitation.</p></article><article><small>PUBLIC ENVIRONMENT</small><p>Recycling station, donation prompt, transportation screen, public-service form, health display, or parking interface.</p></article></div>

A suitable setting has a recognizable decision moment, at least two possible actions including inaction when relevant, one or more visible design features, and an observation method that does not collect identifiable information.

!!! warning "Privacy and ethics boundary"
    Observe the environment, not identifiable people. Do not collect names, student identification numbers, private records, protected-characteristic information, identifiable photographs, faces, covert audio, or covert video. Do not change the environment or conduct an experiment on other people.

## Step 2: Define the decision

Identify the setting, general decision-maker role, decision moment, available actions, and no-action result.

> **Example:** An online university appointment page asks a student to select an in-person or virtual appointment before viewing available times. The student may also leave the page.

## Step 3: Identify one primary choice-architecture feature

<div class="caw-features"><article><small>DEFAULT</small><h3>What happens without a change?</h3><p>Preselection, enrollment, renewal, or a recommendation.</p></article><article><small>ORDER</small><h3>What appears first?</h3><p>Position, sequence, ranking, or prominence.</p></article><article><small>SALIENCE</small><h3>What attracts attention?</h3><p>Color, size, contrast, labels, warnings, or badges.</p></article><article><small>FRICTION</small><h3>What takes more effort?</h3><p>Extra screens, fields, searching, waiting, or opt-out steps.</p></article><article><small>SIMPLIFICATION</small><h3>What reduces complexity?</h3><p>Short lists, grouping, summaries, or recommended paths.</p></article><article><small>SOCIAL INFORMATION</small><h3>What do others appear to do?</h3><p>Ratings, popularity, participation, or testimonials.</p></article><article><small>REFERENCE POINT</small><h3>What anchors comparison?</h3><p>Original prices, suggested amounts, bundles, or side-by-side options.</p></article></div>

## Step 4: Separate evidence from explanation

<div class="caw-evidence"><article><small>OBSERVED</small><p>The recommended option appears first, has a colored border, and is preselected.</p></article><article><small>INTERPRETED</small><p>The arrangement may reduce search and completion effort.</p></article><article><small>NOT ESTABLISHED</small><p>The observation does not prove intent or causal impact.</p></article></div>
