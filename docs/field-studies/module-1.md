## Research question

> How does the visible architecture of a bounded setting appear to make one choice easier to notice, understand, or complete?

## Step 1: Choose a setting

Choose one setting that can be observed without recording or evaluating identifiable people:

- Campus form or sign-up process
- Menu or pricing display
- Website checkout or registration screen
- Recycling or disposal station
- Library or student-service display
- Queue, directional sign, or service process

<div class="m1c-warning"><strong>Do not collect:</strong> names, student IDs, faces, identifiable photographs, private records, protected-characteristic data, covert audio, or covert video.</div>

## Step 2: Record what is visible

<div class="m1c-observe">
  <article><small>OBSERVATION</small><p>The recommended option appears first and is preselected.</p></article>
  <article><small>INTERPRETATION</small><p>The design may reduce search effort or make the recommended response easier.</p></article>
  <article><small>LIMIT</small><p>The observation does not prove designer intent or establish that the design caused behavior.</p></article>
</div>

## Step 4: Write the field note

Write **250–350 words** using these headings:

1. Setting and decision moment
2. Observable architecture
3. Behavior facilitated
4. Behavioral interpretation
5. Alternative explanation
6. Evidence limitation

<div class="m1c-example"><strong>Bounded conclusion</strong><p>The preselected option appears to reduce search and completion effort. This is consistent with a default effect, but the observation does not establish that preselection changes behavior. The ordering may instead reflect administrative workflow.</p></div>
